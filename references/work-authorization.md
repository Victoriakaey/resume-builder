# Work Authorization on the Resume (F-1 / OPT / STEM-OPT / sponsorship)

A dimension that operates **before bullet quality matters**: for an international candidate, a
work-authorization signal (or its absence, or a mishandled portal answer) can screen you out ahead
of the resume read. The skill covers it as a *strategy decision*, not a single rule.

> **Not legal advice.** Immigration specifics change and depend on your case. Treat everything here as
> common career-side guidance to discuss with your school's ISS office / an immigration attorney.
> University ISS pages are authoritative for OPT *rules*; the *presentation* choices below are
> career-side consensus, mostly from resume/career vendors (MED confidence).

## The core decision: disclose vs omit (consensus is genuinely split)
- **Never** write a bare label like "F-1 Visa" / "OPT" with no context — it raises a barrier before
  your qualifications are read.
- **Omit (conservative):** say nothing on the resume; handle work authorization only in the
  application portal's sponsorship question (and, only if the posting explicitly raises it, one
  factual positive line in the cover letter — see `cover-letter.md`).
- **Disclose (proactive):** many recruiters check sponsorship status right after your name; a short,
  *specific, dated* line near contact info can pre-clear an eligibility question rather than leave it
  as a silent risk. If you disclose, be specific — vague beats nothing but specific beats vague.

Pick based on the role and your runway; both are defensible.

## If disclosing — specific, dated phrasings (pick one)
- De-emphasize the ask (best when you have multi-year runway):
  *"Eligible for 36 months of U.S. work authorization via STEM OPT; does not require immediate sponsorship."*
- Full transparency:
  *"Work Authorization: F-1 OPT (valid through Month YYYY). STEM OPT extension eligible. Will require
  H-1B sponsorship for long-term employment."*

Put it in or just below the contact line (within the first ~25 lines), not buried at the bottom.

## The portal trap (highest-stakes, get this right)
Application forms ask a two-part question: **"Do you now OR in the future require sponsorship?"**
- OPT/STEM-OPT itself needs **no** sponsorship — so the "now" is often no.
- But if you'll **later** need H-1B, the honest answer to the combined question is **YES**. Answering
  "no" to dodge a filter is a misrepresentation that surfaces at I-9 / offer stage and can cost the offer.
- This is separate from the resume; the resume can't fix a wrong portal answer, and a right portal
  answer can auto-reject you regardless of a perfect resume. Both matter.

## "No sponsorship" roles
Even though OPT needs no sponsorship, success rates at explicitly "no sponsorship" employers stay low —
the policy usually signals broader institutional resistance to immigration complexity. Don't sink heavy
tailoring effort into them; route effort to sponsor-friendly and startup targets instead.

## Interaction with the ATS
Portal knockout questions (work authorization, location, min-experience) are where the *genuine*
auto-rejects live in modern ATS — not a keyword robot reading your resume (see `resume-craft.md §2`).
So the work-auth answer is a first-class part of the application, not an afterthought.
